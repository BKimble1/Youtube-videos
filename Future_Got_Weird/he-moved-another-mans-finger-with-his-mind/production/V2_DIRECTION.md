# V2 direction (the creator's brief, 4 Nov 2026, verbatim)

FGW FINGER V2 — MAJOR ANIMATION & PACING OVERHAUL

OBJECTIVE

Rebuild the visual execution of FGW_Finger_V1_REVIEW_1920x1080.mp4 into a significantly faster, more cinematic, more visually impressive Future Got Weird episode.

V1 is too slow, static, repetitive, and visually predictable. This is not a small polish pass. V2 must feel like a completely upgraded animation while preserving the approved story.

Keep the approved narration verbatim. Do not rewrite, shorten, reorder, or regenerate narration without approval.

Maintain 1920×1080, 30 fps, the established Weird Crew vector art style, character consistency, and factual accuracy.

1. COMPLETELY REBUILD THE OPENING

The very first frame must immediately show the extraordinary payoff.

Show an extreme close-up of a finger being involuntarily activated, with the other researcher’s brain activity visibly linked to it.

The finger presses the space bar. The cannon fires.

Make the opening instantly understandable and visually exciting.

Within the first five seconds, establish:

* One man is thinking.
* Another man’s finger is moving.
* They are in different buildings.
* The finger fires a cannon in a video game.

Use fast, clean visual storytelling rather than static explanations.

Suggested opening visual sequence:

0:00–0:01: Extreme close-up of the receiving finger snapping downward onto the space bar. Strong tactile motion, keyboard compression, immediate cannon-fire payoff.

0:01–0:02: Smooth push through the keyboard into the game, following the cannon projectile.

0:02–0:03: Match-cut the projectile’s trajectory into a neural signal visualization traveling between the two researchers.

0:03–0:05: Camera pulls outward to reveal the two separate rooms, then smoothly dives into the sender’s EEG cap.

These visual beats must be synchronized to the actual narration. Do not introduce misleading timing or depict the stimulation as voluntary.

2. INCREASE VISUAL DENSITY THROUGHOUT

Target a meaningful visual development approximately every 1–2 seconds, with larger compositional changes approximately every 3–5 seconds when the narration supports them.

This does not mean random cuts.

Use:

* Rapid but readable close-ups
* Medium-to-wide camera reveals
* Purposeful camera tracking
* Foreground/background parallax
* Dynamic character actions
* Object-following camera movement
* Seamless transitions between related concepts
* Multiple distinct shot compositions within each explanation

Avoid holding on a static monitor, chart, building, document, or character while narration continues.

Every visual must contribute to understanding the story.

3. DESIGN INTENTIONAL TRANSITIONS THROUGHOUT

Build a deliberate transition system. Transitions should feel physically connected rather than like unrelated slides.

Use the following opportunities:

Finger → Keyboard → Game

Camera follows the downward finger press, passes through the keyboard surface, and emerges inside the video game as the cannon fires.

Game Projectile → Brain Signal

Track the flying projectile. Its path becomes a neural signal line leading into the researcher’s brain activity.

Brain → EEG Equipment

Push into the brain’s electrical activity, then pull outward to reveal the electrodes and computer interpreting the signal.

Computer Signal → Second Building

Follow the signal through the computer cable, transition into an overhead campus view, then travel toward the receiver’s room.

Receiver’s Head → Finger

Follow the stimulation pathway through a simplified anatomical visualization, transitioning smoothly into the receiver’s real hand.

Finger → Cannon

Use a finger press as the visual trigger for a match-cut to the cannon firing.

Failed Trial → Next Experiment

Instead of cutting to a static session-log document, have the failed game action transform into the session-log result, then transition into the next experiment.

Voting Screens → Shared Decision

Move between participants using connected screen elements, revealing how the responses combine.

These are transition opportunities, not a requirement to force every one into the film. Choose the strongest transitions and space them throughout the entire runtime.

At least one memorable, polished transition should appear in each major story section.

4. SPECIFIC V1 SCENES TO REBUILD

University buildings (~0:19)

The buildings are too static and the composition feels like a basic educational slide.

Replace the long exterior hold with a dynamic campus reveal. Track a visual signal from one room to the other, using foreground buildings for parallax and a smooth camera move into the second room.

Video game (~0:38–0:77)

Avoid repeatedly displaying the same monitor from nearly identical perspectives.

Alternate between the game’s action, close-ups of the researcher, the signal detection, the receiver’s hand, and the cannon.

The viewer should feel the cause-and-effect chain unfolding in real time.

Session log (~1:36)

A static document is visually uninteresting.

Show the failed action first. Then transition into the log as evidence, emphasizing the result without leaving the document on screen for an extended period.

Experimental apparatus (~1:55)

Replace passive equipment shots with an engaging close-up sequence that shows the stimulation equipment, its connection to the receiver, and the resulting movement.

Keep the physical process scientifically accurate.

Trial results (~2:15)

Do not spend excessive time on a stationary three-column diagram.

Animate each trial’s outcome with immediate, readable game action, then assemble the comparison visually.

Voting and shared decision (~2:34–3:15)

This section needs a substantial visual overhaul.

Rather than relying on static YES/NO screens, move between participants, their choices, and the receiver’s game screen.

Show the decisions propagating through the system with clean, visually connected transitions.

5. CAMERA DIRECTION

The camera should feel intentionally directed by a skilled motion designer.

Use smooth acceleration and deceleration, precise framing, and purposeful movement.

Prioritize:

* Strong perspective changes
* Tracking physical actions
* Revealing hidden connections
* Foreground occlusion transitions
* Clean object match-cuts
* Motivated push-ins and pull-outs

Do not create motion by repeatedly zooming in and out of the same illustration.

Do not introduce shaky camera movements, random whip pans, excessive blur, or chaotic cuts.

The animation should feel fast but controlled.

6. CHARACTER AND ANATOMY QUALITY

Maintain the exact Weird Crew visual identity.

All fingers, hands, arms, shoulders, and heads must remain anatomically coherent.

Pay particular attention to:

* Natural finger articulation
* Correct finger-to-keyboard contact
* Plausible arm positions
* Consistent left/right orientation
* No floating hands or disconnected limbs
* No changing character proportions between shots
* No facial features shifting or appearing incorrectly

Because this story revolves around a finger moving, the finger animation must be exceptionally convincing.

7. SCIENTIFIC ACCURACY

The original research must remain accurately represented.

Distinguish EEG measurement, computer signal transmission, and TMS stimulation. Do not depict literal electrical impulses traveling directly through the air between the researchers’ brains.

Any glowing signal pathways should be understood as visual metaphors.

Do not imply the researchers could control arbitrary movements or transmit full thoughts.

Keep the experiments and their outcomes faithful to the verified script.

8. AUDIO AND TIMING

Preserve the approved narration and its wording.

Re-time animation to narration rather than altering the script to accommodate existing visuals.

Use carefully synchronized effects:

* Keyboard press
* Cannon fire
* Game impacts
* Equipment activation
* Subtle signal transitions
* Successful and failed actions

Keep effects restrained enough that the narration remains clear.

No generic overdramatic sound effects on every transition.

9. TECHNICAL IMPLEMENTATION

Inspect the existing Remotion project and reuse established assets, components, and character rigs wherever appropriate.

However, do not preserve weak V1 layouts simply because they already exist.

Rebuild the necessary sequences.

Use smooth interpolation and spring animations with controlled overshoot. Keep character motion physically plausible.

Do not use generic 3D assets, plastic shading, circular AI diagrams, or unnecessary floating UI elements.

Use restrained 2D/2.5D parallax consistent with Future Got Weird.

Before rendering, review the entire video for:

1. A compelling payoff visible in the first frame.
2. No prolonged static scenes.
3. Distinct compositions rather than repeated monitor shots.
4. Smooth, motivated transitions across the entire video.
5. Correct hand and finger anatomy.
6. Accurate synchronization of visual cause and effect.
7. No awkward visual discontinuities.
8. No visual clutter or illegible labels.
9. Consistent characters and art direction.
10. A strong ending that visually reconnects to the opening.
