Japan built a robot with a CHAINSAW for an arm.

And this is real footage of it cutting branches beside a railway.

Watch the other hand. It grabs the branch before the saw touches it.

As the blade cuts through, that claw is still holding the wood.

That's important, because a falling branch and train tracks are a terrible combination.

But now look at the rest of the robot.

It doesn't have legs.

It's attached to a massive boom, rising out of a railway maintenance vehicle.

And it's not deciding where to cut.

A PERSON is controlling every movement.

From a cabin below, the operator watches through the robot's cameras and moves its arms using hand controls.

It looks like a giant humanoid robot, but the actual intelligence is sitting at the bottom.

And those two arms aren't just for show.

One grips the branch and takes its weight.

The other angles the chainsaw into the cut.

As the blade breaks through, the claw can keep holding the branch instead of letting it drop onto the railway.

A crane can lift something. A chainsaw can cut something.

This machine is designed to do BOTH, at the same time, under one person's control.

But there's another piece of engineering you can't see in the footage.

The Japanese robotics company behind this kind of machine developed something called bilateral control.

Tiny movements at the operator's controls command much larger forces at the robot.

And resistance at the robot can be transmitted back through those controls, so the person can actually sense how hard the machine is pushing.

Imagine touching a tree branch with your fingertips.

Except your fingers are now giant STEEL ARMS.

And this isn't just a robotics stunt.

Railway workers have to maintain equipment high above the tracks, sometimes near overhead electrical hardware.

That means climbing, handling heavy tools, and controlling things that can fall.

Moving the operator away from the dangerous part of the job changes the risk.

That's one reason Japan's railways are putting machines like this to work.

JR East introduced this type of machine earlier this year.

And JR West, which was already using a related system in 2024, is rolling out an improved humanoid model with an even longer reach.

It's also introducing a separate single-arm robot built for lifting heavy railway components.

The goal is safer maintenance, with fewer people needed for each job.

And there's STILL a human controlling that chainsaw.

That might actually be the most interesting part.

Japan didn't give a robot a chainsaw and tell it to go find trees.

It gave a PERSON a pair of giant mechanical arms.
