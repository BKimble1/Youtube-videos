## Writing, evidence, and narration standards

- Explain one causal relationship at a time. Introduce a technical term beside the action or object that makes it understandable. An analogy must clarify the mechanism and state its important limit.
- Write conversational sentences that sound natural aloud. Vary sentence length and intensity. Avoid filler, forced slang, breathless hype, and a uniform announcer cadence.
- Use a small number of well-spaced humorous beats; three or four is the starting direction for Video 01. Prefer a visual reaction, dry observation, or callback that supports the explanation.
- Let evidence breathe. Do not solve slow pacing solely by speaking faster, cutting useful context, or moving everything constantly. A clear action, reveal, or change in viewpoint can advance a scene without shortening it.
- Prefer papers, original records, official technical documentation, and direct demonstrations. A vendor is a primary source for its own announcement, not independent validation of the announcement's performance claims. Seek independent replication when that distinction matters.
- Record the exact excerpt, table/page, model/version, testing conditions, and date that support a claim. Published historical answers stay tied to their historical systems; do not substitute current model names.
- Label reconstructed interfaces, toy numbers, simplified diagrams, and fictional characters as illustrative when they could be mistaken for measured evidence. Use real captures for real results.
- Keep full links and required credits in the description and source records. Use concise integrated on-screen context when needed for understanding or attribution.
- Resolve Test Voice by its verified voice ID. Save the selected provider/model/settings and the final audio used. Generate coherent blocks with sufficient context; repair weak takes selectively.
- Maintain a clean displayed script and captions even if synthesis uses pronunciation respellings. Regenerate timing and captions from the final selected audio after a voice change.
- When audio playback is unavailable, identify that limit and leave targeted listening checks. Transcript matching cannot establish warmth, pacing, pronunciation quality, or comic timing.

## Animation and composition standards

Scene action should explain the narration. A character receives a paper, checks a record, reveals an inconsistency, or changes a scoreboard because that action advances the idea. Reusable rigs and assets provide consistency; each episode still needs its own staging.

Use anticipation, contact, reaction, and settle where appropriate. Add secondary motion when it supports the action, then let objects and text rest. Characters should have consistent proportions, costume placement, line weight, silhouettes, hands, and expressions. Preserve already approved characters rather than generating replacements independently for every shot.

Camera movement must have a reason: establish context, follow an action, or reveal a detail. Check the full camera path, not only its first and last frame. Keep essential labels and evidence inside the visible composition throughout the move. Reserve caption space and avoid placing props or overlays over the information being explained.

For real evidence, show enough context to identify the source, then isolate the relevant passage. A full-screen crop with one stable highlight is often clearer than a tiny document card. Test the actual crop at phone size and provide sufficient reading time. Keep editor highlights distinct from the original source.

Environments and props need believable construction. Inspect cabinet doors and hinges, shelf geometry, perspective, hands holding objects, object intersections, masks, joints, and layer order. Correct the specific defect while retaining the approved scene style. Do not add decorative texture or noise that recreates the banner's unwanted line/striation pattern.

For code-driven animation, derive motion from the render timeline; use deterministic randomness where required. Inspect fonts, asset loading, SVG IDs, filters, masks, local versus global timing, scene overlaps, and transitions. Keep a single authoritative audio-driven cue system. Avoid uncontrolled perpetual zooms, abrupt loop resets, and resampling that makes settled text shimmer. Native 30 fps is the recorded baseline; higher frame rate is not a substitute for better motion design.

Use precise code/vector diagrams for information that must be exact. Use generated art selectively for original illustration where appropriate, with consistent references and inspection. Generated imagery must not impersonate a real experiment, screenshot, document, or measured result.

## Sound and delivery standards

Narration leads the mix. Music supports curiosity, tension, or resolution without fighting the voice. Use effects for meaningful physical events and a few deliberate comic beats. Do not add a whoosh to every cut or a sound to every label.

As an internal starting mix target, use approximately −16 to −14 LUFS integrated and true peak at or below −1 dBTP, then judge intelligibility on ordinary speakers and headphones where playback is available. These are production targets, not a statement of platform requirements. Measure the actual output and listen to dense passages, quiet lines, joins, and endings.

Deliver a clearly labelled upload-quality 1080p master and a distinct smaller review preview. Preserve the native timeline frame rate and verify color handling, audio/video duration, codec metadata, and correct version. If a 4K master is requested or appropriate, render it from suitable source assets after the edit settles; do not label an upscale as native 4K. Match subtitles, chapters, description, and thumbnail to that final version.

The requested delivery normally includes final script, claim ledger, source/credit list, timed SRT, selected title and thumbnail, description, chapters, final narration and sound stems, editable source with lockfile, rebuild instructions, and QA notes. Respect the particular task's scope: this setup establishes the system and does not generate those episode files yet.

## Render review and acceptance

| Check | What must be inspected |
|---|---|
| Story and evidence | The hook is fulfilled, the mechanism is understandable, specific claims match sources, and uncertainty is represented fairly |
| Motion | Scene and internal phase boundaries, camera paths, contacts, transitions, loops, flash frames, flicker, and unintended overlaps |
| Composition | Cropping, label alignment, obstructed evidence, aspect/caption space, phone readability, and sufficient reading time |
| Art | Character consistency, hands/props, cabinet/environment geometry, perspective, intersections, and unwanted texture/striations |
| Narration and mix | Requested voice, correct pronunciations, performance, natural pauses, unclipped syllables, matched joins, music ducking, and measured peaks |
| Captions and packaging | Correct names, final timing, accurate chapters, source credits, truthful title/thumbnail, and no private identity in public content |
| Delivery | Actual final version, working files, export properties, durable backup, and reproducible rebuild instructions |

Watch/listen to the entire final render when playback is supported. Inspect risky motion sequences at normal speed and in frame bursts. Still frames and numerical checks are useful supporting evidence but do not replace an audiovisual review. Never claim to have watched or heard something that was not accessible. Report the exact missing checks and leave a focused human review list instead of declaring perfection.

Use a defect log with timecode, symptom, likely source, change, and recheck result. Re-render after meaningful fixes and recheck the affected interval plus any neighboring transitions. Do not repeatedly run broad checks when nothing relevant changed.
