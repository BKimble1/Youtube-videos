# Eel Escape — science and source notes

**Study.** Hasegawa et al., "How Japanese eels escape from the stomach of a predatory fish", *Current Biology*,
September 2024. The team used X-ray videography of juvenile Japanese eels (*Anguilla japonica*) inside the stomachs
of dark sleepers (*Odontobutis obscura*). The eels were injected with a contrast agent so they could be seen once
swallowed. Coverage:
[Live Science](https://livescience.com/animals/fish/watch-an-eel-climb-up-its-predator-s-digestive-tract-and-wriggle-to-freedom-through-its-gills),
[Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2024-09-video-evidence-japanese-eels-predator.html),
[ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/09/240909113034.htm),
[Cosmos](https://cosmosmagazine.com/nature/animals/eels-escape-predator-stomach),
[EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1056826).

**What the Short shows (kept to what the narration says).**

- **The predator.** The fish is a dark sleeper: broad flat head, large mouth, mottled olive-brown, rounded caudal fin,
  resting on a stream bed.
- **The swallowed eel.** It lies in the stomach, swallowed head-first, so its head is deepest.
- **The escape.**
  - The eel reverses tail-first up the esophagus to the throat.
  - The tail turns sideways through a gill slit (between two gill arches), crosses the opercular chamber and comes out
    beneath the gill cover's rear edge.
  - The body follows, and the head comes out last.
  - It pulls its head free and swims away. The predator is unharmed.
- **The X-ray.** It is an illustrated radiograph of the same outline: bright skeleton, gas-dark swim bladder. The eel
  keeps its own colours in every view so it can be followed.

**Stylisation (honest list).**

- **Time.** The real escapes typically took about a minute. The film compresses the escape into about 16 seconds of
  narration.
- **Scale.** The eel is drawn about half the predator's length and a little thicker than life, so it reads at phone size.
- **Path.** The route is idealised as a single clean path. Some eels in the study first circled the stomach, and not
  all reached the gills.
- **The rewind.** The brief "visual rewind" is an editorial device: the opening shows the tail already out, and the
  rewind returns the eel to the stomach before the escape is played forward.
- **The ending.** The departing tail's sweep past the lens is a loop device.

**Research footage.** The study's X-ray video is not in this repository, and no reuse clearance is on file. So,
as the brief allows, the X-ray beat uses the channel's own illustration, not the footage.
