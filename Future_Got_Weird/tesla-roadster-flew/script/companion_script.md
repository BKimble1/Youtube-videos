## B3 · Upside down (≈0:58–1:43)
> Start with the upside-down trick, because it's the cleverest. Under that ramp, gravity pulls the car away with its
> full weight. That's one g. To stay put, something has to push back harder. Tesla says fans pull air out from under
> the car. That leaves a thin layer of low-pressure air between the floor and the ramp, while the air on the other
> side stays at normal pressure… and normal air pressure is surprisingly strong. The difference shoves the car into
> the surface. Tesla claims the system can pull up to two g: double what it needs to hang there. Racing teams tried
> fan cars back in the 1970s, and they gripped so well that the rules shut them down. And notice: this suction is a
> separate system from the jets.

## B4 · In the air (≈1:43–2:30)
> Now the jets. These are cold-gas thrusters: a tank of compressed gas, some valves, and nozzles. No fuel. No flame.
> Push gas out one way, and the car gets pushed the other way, like letting go of a balloon. Fire a nozzle up on one
> side and down on the other, and the car rolls. Fire the opposite pair, and the roll stops. Tesla says it worked
> with SpaceX to make thrusters like these work in air, not just in space. Watch the launch again: the ramp provides
> the speed and the height, and the jets help manage the rotation and the landing. What none of this shows is
> sustained flight, a useful flying range, or anything certified to carry people through the air.
