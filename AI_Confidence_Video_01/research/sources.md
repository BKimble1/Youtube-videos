## Things we must not claim

### The opening example and the capability contrast

1. That the IMO gold system and the Table 1 models are the same, or that "AI" in general does both. Label each with its own system, company and date.
2. That today's ChatGPT, DeepSeek or Llama, or any search-enabled product, gives these answers. Table 1 is a May 2025 snapshot without web search.
3. That Table 1 or the birthday example shows an error rate ("AI is wrong X% of the time"). They are 1 prompt per model, and 3 tries on 1 model.
4. That all three got the university wrong. GPT-4o named CMU.
5. That the excerpt is the beginning or the whole of a reply. Change s08 "Here's how ChatGPT's began" to "Here's part of ChatGPT's answer".
6. That the birthday test and Table 1 used the same model. The birthday test was DeepSeek-V3 on 11 May; Table 1 used DeepSeek-R1 on 9 May.
7. Kalai's real birthday, or any guess at it. The paper gives only "Autumn".
8. That Adam Kalai personally typed the prompts. The paper doesn't say; call him the paper's lead author.
9. "A completely different title" (s21). The titles share words.
10. That Kalai's thesis title is absent from the training data, or appears exactly once. That is unknown.

### Mechanism

11. That next-token scores shown on screen are real model probabilities, unless we generate and log them. Otherwise label them "illustrative".
12. That the token split shown is what chatgpt.com computed on 2025-05-09. It is GPT-4o's public tokenizer applied to the published excerpt.
13. That it's "just autocomplete", or that next-word prediction alone causes hallucinations. Kalai et al. reject that framing "from a purely statistical perspective, ignoring computation" (p. 6, "Not merely autocomplete"), while noting that architecture "can introduce additional errors".
14. That likelihood is unrelated to truth. For common facts it usually tracks truth: Kalai p. 10 asserts this without data, and GPT-4's pre-trained model was well calibrated on an MMLU subset (row 21). Equally, never present a next-token probability as "the chance this is true".
15. That models "lie", "know they're lying", "try to deceive" or "have no idea". Avoid claims about intent in either direction.

### The incentive argument

16. That binary grading is proven to cause hallucinations, or is the only cause. Say "the researchers argue … one contributing explanation".
17. That AI companies deliberately train models to bluff or lie.
